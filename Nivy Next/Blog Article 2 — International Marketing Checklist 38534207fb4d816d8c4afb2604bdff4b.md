# International Marketing Checklist: US & UK Market Readiness

Most growth-stage businesses expanding into the US or UK make the same assumption: if the offer works at home, it'll work abroad with a few tweaks — change the currency symbol, swap a few words, maybe localise the spelling. Then the campaigns launch, the cost per click is higher than expected, conversion rates lag, and nobody can quite explain why.

The reason is usually simple. International expansion isn't a translation exercise. It's a market-readiness exercise — and most of the readiness work happens in places founders don't think to look: compliance, search infrastructure, trust signals, and payment friction, not just messaging.

## Why "Translate and Launch" Doesn't Work

A campaign that converts well domestically is the product of dozens of small, invisible trust signals built up over time — familiar currency, familiar payment methods, local proof points, search visibility on the right domain. Drop that same campaign into a new market and every one of those signals resets to zero. Buyers in an unfamiliar market default to caution, which means the burden of proof is higher, not lower, than it was at home.

This shows up most clearly in two markets growth-stage companies expand into first: the United States and the United Kingdom. They share a language, which creates a false sense of similarity. But they differ sharply on compliance requirements, search behaviour, advertising cost benchmarks, and — perhaps most underestimated — the amount of trust signal UK buyers expect before they'll convert at all.

## The Market Readiness Checklist

**1. Compliance & Legal Infrastructure**

- GDPR consent language on every form (required for any UK or EU visitor, regardless of where your business is based)
- A live, linked Privacy Policy naming your actual data processors (analytics, CRM, payment, scheduling tools)
- Cookie consent banner with a genuine opt-out, not just a dismiss button
- CCPA awareness if you're collecting data from California residents — disclosure and opt-out rights apply even to non-US companies serving US customers
- A clear data retention policy you can state in plain language if asked

**2. Search & Organic Visibility**

- Confirm whether you're being indexed and served on the correct regional domain ([Google.co.uk](http://Google.co.uk) behaves differently from [Google.com](http://Google.com) in what it surfaces for the same query)
- Run keyword research separately per market — terminology, search intent, and competitor density all shift even within English-language markets
- Local SEO signals (business listings, local schema, regional backlinks) if you're targeting city- or region-specific buyers in the UK

**3. Paid Channels & Cost Benchmarks**

- Pull current CPC and CPM benchmarks for your category in each target market before setting budget — US and UK costs frequently differ by a wide margin, and a budget that's competitive in one market can be priced out of the other
- Localise ad copy beyond spelling: currency display, units of measurement, and cultural reference points all affect click-through and conversion
- Set market-specific conversion goals rather than averaging performance across markets — a blended view of US and UK ad performance hides which market is actually working

**4. Content & Tone**

- British English vs. American English spelling and phrasing — small, but it's one of the fastest ways a UK visitor identifies a site as "not built for me"
- Calibrate trust-signal density to the market. UK buyers, in our experience, expect more proof — case studies, certifications, named contacts — before they'll convert, compared to the same offer presented to a US audience
- Avoid US-centric idioms and sports metaphors in copy aimed at UK or Australian audiences; they read as foreign rather than relatable

**5. Conversion Infrastructure**

- Display pricing in the visitor's local currency, or at minimum state clearly that local-currency equivalents are available on request
- Offer payment methods buyers actually expect in that market (Stripe and wire transfer cover most of the US and UK; some EU buyers still expect bank transfer as the default)
- Show timezone-aware contact information and response commitments — a UK visitor wants to know you'll respond within their business hours, not somewhere on the other side of the planet
- Regional contact details (even a regional email alias) measurably reduce the "who am I actually talking to" hesitation for international buyers

## A Practical Readiness Checklist

Before launching a campaign in a new market, confirm you can check every box below:

- [ ]  GDPR consent checkbox live on every form
- [ ]  Privacy Policy published and linked in the footer, with real data processors named
- [ ]  Market-specific keyword research completed (not reused from your home market)
- [ ]  Local currency or currency-equivalent messaging in place
- [ ]  At least one trust signal (certification, testimonial, or case study) visible near every CTA
- [ ]  Timezone-aware response commitment stated clearly
- [ ]  Region-appropriate spelling and tone reviewed by someone familiar with that market

If more than one or two boxes are unchecked, the campaign is likely to underperform regardless of how strong the creative or targeting is — the friction is structural, not creative.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

When we bring a client into a new market, market-readiness work happens during the Strategy & Scoping phase, before a single ad goes live — alongside the Discovery Call and well before Execution begins. It's deliberately front-loaded, because retrofitting compliance language or trust signals after a campaign has already launched costs more in wasted spend than building it correctly the first time.

The goal isn't perfection before launch. It's making sure the things that quietly kill conversion — a missing GDPR checkbox, an unfamiliar currency, a tone that reads as foreign — are handled before traffic starts arriving, not discovered three weeks into a campaign when the numbers don't add up.

## The Bottom Line

International expansion fails far more often on infrastructure than on offer. The product is usually fine. The market readiness around it — compliance, search, payment, tone, trust — is what determines whether that offer gets a fair hearing. Run through the checklist above before your next market launch, and you'll fix the expensive mistakes on paper instead of in ad spend.
