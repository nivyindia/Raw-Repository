# Expat Finance: Managing Your Money Across Borders

Expat tax is one conversation. Expat finance is a bigger one. The tax question — what do I owe, where — is answerable with the right filing support. The financial question is harder: what happens to everything I've already built (a 401(k), a UK pension, a property, savings in a currency I no longer live in) once I move, and how do I make decisions about those things intelligently when two countries' rules apply at once?

This guide covers the financial management questions that come up for internationally mobile individuals — not just what you file, but what you own across borders and how it actually works once you leave.

---

## Why Expat Finance Is Broader Than Expat Tax

Tax compliance is the floor. You file what you owe, you meet the deadlines, you avoid the penalties. That's necessary but it's not the whole picture. The financial decisions that move money — whether to keep contributing to a retirement account, whether to transfer a pension, where to accumulate savings, how to structure a cross-border asset base — all feed into your tax position but exist independently of it.

The gap between the two is where most individually mobile people run into trouble: they sort out the compliance side (often reluctantly, after a penalty) without ever properly addressing the financial structure underneath it. A UK national who moves to the US and leaves their pension unexamined doesn't just have a filing oversight — they can have a structural mismatch that costs materially more than the penalty it would have generated.

The decisions covered in this guide all share one feature: they're easier, cheaper, and more flexible to make before you move (or immediately after), and progressively harder to reverse the longer they're left.

## Retirement Accounts Across Borders

This is where the financial impact of moving is most immediate for most people. Retirement accounts are jurisdiction-specific by design: a 401(k) is a US construct, a UK pension is a UK construct, and neither one translates cleanly into the other country's tax framework once the holder is living on the wrong side of the border.

### US 401(k) for Expats

The core issue for US expats with a 401(k) is the interaction between the account's tax benefit and how income is being sheltered abroad. A 401(k) contribution is pre-tax — it reduces US taxable income. But if an expat is using the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** to exclude foreign-earned income from US tax, there may be little US taxable income left to reduce in the first place. The contribution's main benefit evaporates if the income it would have offset is already excluded.

Using the **Foreign Tax Credit** instead of FEIE changes the picture: the credit offsets US tax dollar-for-dollar based on local tax paid, leaving more US taxable income that a 401(k) contribution can meaningfully reduce. Which approach is optimal isn't a one-time choice — it changes year to year based on the host country's tax rate and income level.

For expats who still have access to a US employer's 401(k) plan (typically: working remotely for a US employer), contributions can continue exactly as if they were in the US, subject to the FEIE/credit interaction above. Expats working for foreign employers generally lose access to contribute — a 401(k) is tied to US payroll. Existing balances continue to grow tax-deferred under US rules regardless of where you live; Required Minimum Distributions apply on the same US schedule. Early withdrawal penalties don't disappear because you've moved.

The key variable most people miss: some host countries don't recognize US tax-deferred status at all, and tax 401(k) growth or contributions currently under local law — making the calculation genuinely two-sided, not just a US question.

### UK Pensions for Expats

For UK nationals moving abroad, the fundamental choice is leave-it-or-transfer-it — and it's more consequential than it looks.

**Leaving a UK pension in place** means it continues under UK rules: tax-free growth, withdrawals taxable in the UK as pension income (though the UK-host country tax treaty may reallocate taxing rights once you're a confirmed non-resident). The 25% UK tax-free lump sum (Pension Commencement Lump Sum) is a UK concept — some destination countries don't recognize it as tax-free under their own rules, and the same lump sum can be taxed locally even when it wasn't taxed in the UK.

**Transferring via a Qualifying Recognised Overseas Pension Scheme (QROPS)** is possible but carries real risk. The Overseas Transfer Charge can apply (currently 25% in many cases) unless specific conditions are met — broadly, where you and the receiving scheme are in the same country. Transferring to a non-qualifying scheme can trigger an unauthorized payment charge of up to 55% of the transferred value. Once transferred, UK pension protections generally don't follow the funds.

For UK nationals moving to the **US specifically**, a SIPP can trigger US reporting obligations under foreign trust rules, and the timing mismatch between UK tax-free growth and US taxation of that same growth is a recurring source of unplanned US tax exposure — covered in our US-UK treaty article.

## Holding Company and Structuring Considerations for HNW Individuals

For individuals with significant wealth or cross-border income sources — founders who've had a liquidity event, professionals with income in multiple countries, individuals planning generational transfer — entity structuring at the personal level often makes sense in a way it doesn't for straightforward employed expats.

The most common vehicle in this space is a **Singapore holding structure**: Singapore's territorial tax system (foreign-sourced income generally untaxed unless remitted), no capital gains tax, no outbound dividend withholding, and 90+ tax treaties collectively make it the most-used hub jurisdiction for cross-border asset accumulation. That reputation is earned — but it's also frequently misapplied.

The critical qualifier: treaty benefits and territorial treatment aren't available just because an entity is registered in Singapore. **Genuine economic substance** is required — real management decisions made in Singapore, not a registered address and a nominee director. Tax authorities in the underlying subsidiary's home country can challenge a Singapore structure that exists only on paper and disregard it entirely, taxing the income as if the Singapore layer never existed.

For smaller wealth levels where the overhead of maintaining a Singapore entity (incorporation, ongoing substance documentation, local directorship, annual filings) would exceed the tax saved on expected dividend or capital gains flows, the holding layer is overhead without the benefit. The case for Singapore holding structure strengthens when there are multiple operating subsidiaries, significant cross-border dividend flows, and a genuine exit or reinvestment horizon where the territorial system's treatment of capital gains makes a material difference.

## Banking and Currency Practicalities

This is the part of expat financial life that has the most operational friction and the least professional support. Tax advisors handle filings; no one typically handles the messy middle of multi-currency personal finance.

**Multi-currency personal accounts** — Wise, Revolut, and similar services solve the practical problem of holding and moving money across currencies without the spread and delay of traditional bank FX. They don't solve the reporting side: foreign financial accounts above USD 10,000 at any point in the year trigger FBAR obligations for US persons. Foreign bank accounts above $50,000 at year-end or $75,000 at any point trigger FATCA Form 8938 for US persons. Neither threshold is indexed to inflation, and both apply to the aggregate balance across all foreign accounts, not per account.

**FX timing decisions** — Converting savings or income between currencies introduces FX gain/loss that can be reportable under home-country tax rules, depending on the amount and the country. A UK resident converting USD savings to GBP can realise a foreign currency gain that's chargeable under UK capital gains rules. The rules aren't consistent across countries, and "I just moved money between accounts" is not how tax authorities read the transaction.

**Repatriation planning** — For expats planning to eventually return to their home country, how and when savings accumulated in the host country get repatriated is a planning question, not just a logistics one. Some structures shelter repatriation; some create unexpected tax events. The decisions made during the abroad period determine the options available when coming home.

## Common Misconceptions

**"I'm not a resident anywhere."** This is the most expensive misconception in expat finance, and the most common among digital nomads and frequent movers. Tax residency isn't determined by where you don't live; it's determined by where each country's rules say you *do* live, and those rules are asymmetric. Several countries — the US most prominently — assert tax jurisdiction based on citizenship, not residency. Others apply day-count tests that catch people who assume "I was only there 60 days" means no nexus. "Not a resident anywhere" isn't a real tax position; it's a gap that two or more countries' rules will each try to fill independently.

**"My local bank account means local tax simplicity."** Opening a bank account in a new country doesn't simplify your home-country reporting obligations — for US persons, it adds them. A UK expat in the US opening a UK Barclays account for UK obligations doesn't reduce their US filing requirements; it adds FBAR and potentially Form 8938 obligations for that account. The administrative load goes up, not down, when you hold accounts in multiple jurisdictions.

**"I'll sort the pension/401k when I move back."** Some decisions are reversible; some aren't. A QROPS transfer that triggers an unauthorized payment charge, a 401(k) early withdrawal taken because of liquidity pressure abroad, or a retirement account left in a jurisdiction that imposes tax on the growth years before the account is touched — these all have real costs that a "we'll fix it later" approach doesn't prevent. The decisions that are easiest to make are at the start, before filing positions are locked in and before account structures become difficult to unwind.

---

## What This Means in Practice

The common thread across retirement accounts, holding structures, banking, and residency questions is the same: the decisions that preserve optionality, reduce friction, and avoid unexpected tax events are almost always made *before* the move (or in the first year, before positions harden), not after several years of implicit choices about what to do with existing accounts and accumulated savings.

Getting this right isn't about minimising tax — it's about not creating tax problems inadvertently through decisions that feel administrative at the time. The 401(k) contribution question, the pension transfer decision, the holding structure for a liquidity event: none of these feel like high-stakes financial decisions when you're dealing with them. They become high-stakes retroactively.
