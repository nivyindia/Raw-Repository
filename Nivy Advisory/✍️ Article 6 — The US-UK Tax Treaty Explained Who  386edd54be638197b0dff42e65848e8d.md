# The US-UK Tax Treaty Explained: Who Gets Taxed First?

If you're a US citizen living in the UK, a UK national with US income, or anyone holding assets on both sides of the Atlantic, you've probably been told "there's a treaty for that" — as if the treaty makes the problem go away. It doesn't. It allocates the problem. The US-UK tax treaty decides which country taxes a given type of income first, and what relief the other country has to give you so you're not taxed twice. Getting that allocation right is where most of the value (and most of the mistakes) live.

## The Basic Mechanism

Neither the US nor the UK gives up its right to tax you just because a treaty exists. Instead, the treaty:

- Assigns **primary taxing rights** to one country for each income type (employment income, dividends, pensions, capital gains, real estate)
- Requires the *other* country to give you a **foreign tax credit** or **exemption** so the same dollar isn't taxed twice
- Sets **tie-breaker rules** for determining which country you're a tax resident of, when both countries' domestic rules would otherwise claim you

The practical effect: you almost always still have to *file* in both countries, even when you don't end up *owing* in one of them. Filing and owing are separate questions, and the treaty only really helps with the second one.

## The Part That Catches US Citizens Specifically: the Saving Clause

Most tax treaties let a resident of one country reduce their tax obligations to the other. The US-UK treaty includes a **saving clause** that lets the US continue taxing its own citizens on worldwide income *regardless* of treaty benefits that would otherwise apply. In plain terms: being a UK resident doesn't get a US citizen out of US filing obligations, treaty or no treaty. This is the single most common point of confusion for Americans who've lived in the UK for years and assumed the treaty "covered" them.

## How Specific Income Types Are Treated

| Income Type | General Treaty Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **Employment income** | Taxed where the work is physically performed, with limited exceptions for short-term assignments |
| **Dividends** | Source country can withhold a reduced rate (typically 15% under the treaty, vs. higher statutory rates); residence country gives credit |
| **Pensions** | Generally taxed only in the country of residence — but UK Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs) and US 401(k)/IRA accounts have specific treaty articles governing timing and treatment that don't always match common assumptions |
| **Capital gains (real estate)** | Generally taxable in the country where the property sits, in addition to the residence country's tax (with credit relief) |
| **Capital gains (shares/investments)** | Generally taxed only in the country of residence — a frequent source of confusion since real estate and shares are treated differently |

## Where This Gets Genuinely Complicated

**Pension timing mismatches.** The US and UK don't tax retirement account growth and withdrawals on the same schedule. A UK pension that grows tax-free in the UK may still trigger US reporting and, in some cases, current US taxation under US tax rules for foreign trusts — a SIPP can sometimes be treated this way if not structured carefully. This is not a place to guess.

**Residency tie-breakers.** If you split time between the US and UK, both countries' domestic residency tests might independently claim you as a resident. The treaty's tie-breaker rules (permanent home, center of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality, in that order) resolve the conflict — but applying them requires a documented position, not just an assumption.

**Self-employment and business income.** A self-employed person with clients in both countries needs to determine where a "permanent establishment" exists, which affects which country has primary taxing rights on business profits.

## What This Means in Practice

If you're navigating both sides of this treaty, the work isn't choosing between "file in the US" or "file in the UK" — it's filing correctly in both, claiming the right credits or exclusions on each return, and making treaty elections (where available) that put you in the best position once both returns are considered together. This is exactly the kind of problem a single-country advisor structurally can't solve well: a US-only CPA doesn't know your UK position, and a UK-only accountant isn't licensed to advise on US tax law.
