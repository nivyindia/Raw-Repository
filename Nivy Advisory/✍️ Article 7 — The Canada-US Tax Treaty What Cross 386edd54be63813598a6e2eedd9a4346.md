# The Canada-US Tax Treaty: What Cross-Border Individuals Actually Need to Know

Canada and the US share the longest land border in the world and one of the most heavily used tax treaties — which is exactly why so many cross-border Canadians and Americans assume it handles more than it actually does. The Canada-US Tax Convention prevents double taxation. It does not prevent double *filing*, and it does not make either country fully step aside.

## The Basic Mechanism

Like most tax treaties, the Canada-US treaty:

- Assigns **primary taxing rights** by income type (employment, pensions, dividends, capital gains, real property)
- Provides **foreign tax credit** relief so income taxed in one country reduces the tax owed on the same income in the other
- Sets **residency tie-breaker rules** for people who could be considered tax resident in both countries simultaneously

The most important thing to understand up front: **if you're a US citizen, you file a US return regardless of where you live.** This is the treaty's "saving clause" at work — the same mechanism that exists in the US-UK treaty. Canadian residency, Canadian tax payments, and the treaty itself do not eliminate the US filing obligation for US citizens. They reduce or eliminate double *taxation*, which is a different thing entirely.

## How Specific Income Types Are Treated

| Income Type | General Treaty Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **Employment income** | Generally taxed where work is physically performed, with a short-term exception (typically under 183 days and under a compensation threshold) |
| **Dividends** | Source country withholding capped at 15% under the treaty (often 5% for substantial corporate shareholders); residence country gives credit |
| **Pensions and Social Security/CPP** | Generally taxed primarily by the source country, with the residence country providing relief — but the mechanics differ meaningfully between RRSPs, US 401(k)/IRA accounts, CPP, and US Social Security |
| **Capital gains (real property)** | Taxable in the country where the property is located, in addition to residence-country tax (credit relief applies) |
| **Capital gains (shares/investments)** | Generally taxed only in the country of residence |

## Where This Gets Genuinely Complicated

**RRSP treatment for US persons.** A Canadian RRSP grows tax-deferred in Canada. Historically, US persons had to make an annual election to preserve that deferral for US tax purposes; current rules have simplified this considerably, but US reporting obligations for foreign accounts (FBAR, FATCA) still apply to RRSPs regardless of the income tax treatment. Account structure and reporting are two separate questions — treating them as one is a common and expensive mistake.

**CCPC and corporate structuring.** A Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC) gets preferential tax treatment (the Small Business Deduction) — but loses that status the moment it's controlled by non-residents, including US shareholders past certain thresholds. Cross-border business owners need to structure ownership with this in mind from incorporation, not discover it at tax time.

**The departure tax.** Canada imposes a deemed disposition ("departure tax") on most property when an individual ceases to be a Canadian tax resident — effectively taxing unrealized gains as if the assets were sold the day you left. This catches people who think of "leaving Canada" as a simple administrative change rather than a taxable event.

**Totalization and CPP/Social Security.** A separate US-Canada Social Security agreement (distinct from the tax treaty) determines which country's social security system you pay into and which retirement benefits you're eligible for — relevant for anyone who has worked across both countries during their career.

## What This Means in Practice

The treaty's real value shows up when both returns are planned together: timing RRSP withdrawals against US tax brackets, sequencing the departure tax against US cost-basis rules, or structuring a cross-border business to avoid losing CCPC status unintentionally. None of that happens if a Canadian accountant files the Canadian return and a separate US CPA files the US return without ever comparing notes.
