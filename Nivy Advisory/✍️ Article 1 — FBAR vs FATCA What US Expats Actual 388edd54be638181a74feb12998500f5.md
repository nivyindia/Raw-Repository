# FBAR vs. FATCA: What US Expats Actually Need to File

Two forms, two agencies, two sets of thresholds — and one very common mistake: assuming that filing one covers the other. If you're a US citizen or Green Card holder with financial accounts outside the US, FBAR and FATCA are both probably relevant to you, and they are not the same filing wearing two names. Here's what actually needs to go where.

## Why These Two Get Confused

Both FBAR and FATCA exist to do roughly the same thing: tell the US government about foreign financial accounts and assets held by US persons. That shared purpose is exactly why people assume they're interchangeable. They aren't. They go to different agencies, use different forms, apply different thresholds, and carry different penalty structures. Filing one with no awareness of the other is one of the most common — and most expensive — gaps in expat tax compliance.

## FBAR: FinCEN Form 114

FBAR (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts) is filed with the **Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)**, a bureau of the Treasury Department — not the IRS.

- **Who must file:** Any US person (citizen, Green Card holder, or US resident) with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts
- **Threshold:** The aggregate value of all foreign accounts exceeded **$10,000 at any point during the year** — not per account, and not on December 31 specifically. Three accounts with $4,000 each, even briefly, trigger the requirement
- **What counts as an account:** Bank accounts, brokerage accounts, mutual funds, and certain foreign pension accounts — even ones with no US tax consequence on their own
- **Deadline:** April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15 (no extension request needed)
- **Filed how:** Electronically, directly with FinCEN — separate from your tax return entirely

## FATCA: Form 8938

FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act) reporting is done via **Form 8938**, filed as part of your federal income tax return with the **IRS**.

- **Who must file:** US persons whose total foreign financial assets exceed a threshold that varies by filing status and residency
- **Threshold (2024 figures, confirm current year before publishing):**
    - Single filers living in the US: assets over $50,000 at year-end, or $75,000 at any point during the year
    - Single filers living abroad: assets over $200,000 at year-end, or $300,000 at any point during the year
    - Married filing jointly abroad: double the above figures
- **What counts as an asset:** Broader than FBAR — includes foreign stock, foreign mutual funds, foreign-issued life insurance with cash value, and interests in foreign entities, in addition to accounts
- **Deadline:** Same as your tax return — April 15, with extensions following your 1040 extension (including the automatic June 15 extension for filers abroad)
- **Filed how:** Attached to your Form 1040

## Side-by-Side

| — | FBAR (FinCEN 114) | FATCA (Form 8938) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Agency** | FinCEN / Treasury | IRS |
| **Threshold** | $10,000 aggregate, any time in the year | $50,000–$300,000+, depends on filing status & residency |
| **Scope** | Financial accounts only | Accounts + broader foreign assets |
| **Filed with** | Standalone, direct to FinCEN | Attached to Form 1040 |
| **Most filers need** | Often both — they are not mutually exclusive | Often both — they are not mutually exclusive |

The most important row in that table is the last one. Many expats with meaningful foreign account balances are required to file **both** — FBAR because the aggregate cleared $10,000, and FATCA because their total foreign assets cleared the higher threshold. Filing only one doesn't satisfy the other; they're assessed and penalized independently.

## What Happens If You've Missed Prior Years

If you've discovered you should have been filing FBAR, FATCA, or both for prior years, the IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures exist specifically for **non-willful** failures to file. Two versions apply depending on your situation:

- **Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures** — for US persons who didn't meet the substantial presence test for one or more of the last three years (most expats fall here)
- **Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures** — for US persons who did meet the residency test, with a modest penalty attached

Acting before the IRS contacts you matters. Once the IRS has opened an inquiry, the Streamlined door generally closes, and penalty exposure increases significantly — non-willful FBAR penalties alone can reach $10,000 per violation, per year, and willful penalties are far higher.

## What This Means in Practice

The question isn't "FBAR or FATCA" — it's "do I clear either threshold, and if so, which forms do I actually need this year." That assessment depends on account balances throughout the year (not just at year-end), filing status, and residency — details that are easy to get wrong without walking through the thresholds deliberately every filing season.
