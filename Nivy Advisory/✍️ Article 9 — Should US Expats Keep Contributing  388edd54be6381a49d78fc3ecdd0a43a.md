# Should US Expats Keep Contributing to a 401(k) From Abroad?

This is one of the highest-volume questions US expats search for, and it has almost zero straightforward coverage — because the honest answer is "it depends on how you're earning abroad," not a clean yes or no. Here's the actual mechanics behind that dependency.

## Why This Question Even Comes Up

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored US retirement plan. The moment you move abroad, two things can change at once: whether you have access to a US-sponsored 401(k) at all, and whether contributing to it still makes sense given how the rest of your US tax picture is affected by living overseas. Most expats discover the question matters only when they're deciding whether to keep an old employer's plan, roll it over, or — if working for a US employer remotely — keep contributing as normal.

## The Core Mechanic: Contribution Limits Don't Disappear, But Your Income Picture Might Change

A 401(k) contribution is typically made pre-tax, reducing your taxable US income for the year. For an expat using the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** to exclude foreign-earned income from US tax, this creates an interaction worth understanding clearly:

- If your income is **excluded under FEIE**, you've already removed it from US taxable income — a 401(k) contribution's main tax benefit (reducing taxable income) has less to offset, because there may be little or no US taxable income left to reduce
- If you're using the **Foreign Tax Credit** instead of FEIE (common when local tax rates are high enough that the credit shelters more), your US taxable income picture looks different, and a 401(k) contribution can still meaningfully reduce US tax owed

Which approach — FEIE or Foreign Tax Credit — you're using isn't a one-time choice either; it interacts with this decision every year, and switching between them carries its own rules and consequences.

## Can You Even Contribute From Abroad?

Whether contributing is even possible depends entirely on your employment situation:

- **Working remotely for a US employer** that still sponsors a 401(k): contributions generally continue exactly as if you were in the US, subject to the FEIE/credit interaction above
- **Working for a foreign employer**: you generally lose access to contribute to a US 401(k) altogether, since it's an employer-sponsored plan tied to US payroll
- **Self-employed abroad**: a 401(k) in the traditional sense isn't available, but self-employed US persons abroad may have access to other US retirement vehicles depending on their specific structure

## What Happens to an Existing 401(k) Balance While You're Abroad

For expats who already have a 401(k) from prior US employment, the balance itself doesn't require any action just because you've moved — it continues to grow tax-deferred under US rules regardless of where you live. The decisions that do come up:

- **Leaving it with the former employer's plan** vs. **rolling it into an IRA** — rollover rules are largely unaffected by living abroad, but available investment options and account access can be more limited from overseas depending on the provider
- **Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)** still apply on the same US schedule once you reach the applicable age, regardless of residency
- **Early withdrawal penalties** still apply under the same rules — living abroad doesn't create an exception to the 10% early withdrawal penalty before age 59½

## Where This Gets Genuinely Complicated

The FEIE-versus-Foreign-Tax-Credit interaction above is the single biggest variable, because it changes year to year based on your foreign tax rate and income level — a decision that looks optimal one year can flip the next. Layer on a host country's own tax treatment of US retirement accounts (some countries don't recognize the US tax-deferred status at all, taxing 401(k) growth or contributions currently under local law) and the calculation becomes genuinely two-sided, not just a US-side question.

## What This Means in Practice

The answer isn't a blanket "yes, keep contributing" or "no, stop" — it depends on whether you're using FEIE or the Foreign Tax Credit this year, what your host country does with US retirement accounts under its own tax rules, and whether you even have access to contribute given your employment structure abroad. This is exactly the kind of decision that needs to be modeled against your specific numbers each year, not answered once and left alone.
