# UK Self-Assessment for Non-Residents: What Changes When You Move Abroad

Moving abroad doesn't automatically close your UK tax file. HMRC doesn't send a closing notice when you leave, and a lot of people only find out they still have obligations when a letter — or a penalty — arrives. Here's what actually changes when you leave the UK, and what quietly doesn't.

## Do You Still Need to File After Leaving the UK?

Leaving the UK ends your UK *residence* for tax purposes only once you can demonstrate it under HMRC's rules — it does not end your UK tax *obligations* on UK-sourced income. Even as a confirmed non-resident, you generally still owe UK tax (and a Self-Assessment return) on:

- **UK rental income** — from any UK property, regardless of where you live
- **UK pension income** — though treaty relief may reduce or eliminate UK tax depending on your new country of residence
- **UK-sourced dividends and interest** in some circumstances
- **Gains on UK residential property**, even for non-residents, under the Non-Resident Capital Gains Tax rules

What does change is the *scope* — once non-resident, you're generally no longer taxed on your worldwide income, only UK-sourced income. But "no longer taxed on everything" is not the same as "no longer required to file anything," and that distinction is where most people trip up.

## Split-Year Treatment Basics

In the tax year you actually move, you don't necessarily count as either fully resident or fully non-resident for the whole year. HMRC's **split-year treatment** divides that tax year into a UK part and an overseas part, so you're only taxed as a UK resident for the portion before you left (and vice versa if you're arriving).

Split-year treatment isn't automatic — it only applies if you meet one of several specific cases set out in the Statutory Residence Test legislation (for example, starting full-time work overseas, or your partner doing so). Getting this wrong in either direction either overstates your UK tax bill or understates it — and understating it is the version that creates a problem later.

## Common Triggers That Keep Non-Residents in the Self-Assessment System

Even confirmed non-residents often still need to file because of:

- **A UK rental property** — the single most common reason. You can register for the **Non-Resident Landlord (NRL) scheme** to receive rent gross instead of having 20% withheld by the agent or tenant, but registering for NRL does not remove the Self-Assessment filing requirement — it just changes how the tax is collected during the year
- **UK self-employment or partnership income** that continues after you leave
- **Capital gains on UK property** disposed of while non-resident — these have their own 60-day reporting deadline, separate from the annual Self-Assessment timeline
- **Untaxed UK investment income** above the dividend or savings allowance
- **Claiming split-year treatment itself** — which is claimed *on* a Self-Assessment return, so the return that gets you out of full UK taxation is the same return you still have to file

## Deadlines That Don't Change Just Because You've Moved

This is the part that catches people most often: HMRC's filing deadlines are not extended or adjusted for non-residents.

- **31 October** — paper Self-Assessment deadline (same for residents and non-residents)
- **31 January** — online Self-Assessment filing and payment deadline
- **60 days** from completion — UK property disposal reporting and CGT payment for non-residents, a separate and faster deadline than the annual return

Living in a different time zone, dealing with a new country's onboarding, or simply not having a UK postal address does not move these dates. Penalties accrue on the same schedule regardless of where you're living when the deadline passes — an immediate £100 penalty for missing the January deadline, with further penalties accruing at 3, 6, and 12 months.

## The Statutory Residence Test Sits Behind All of This

Whether any of the above even applies to you in full depends on your residency status, which HMRC determines through the **Statutory Residence Test (SRT)** — a structured set of day-count thresholds and connecting factors (UK ties such as family, accommodation, and work) rather than a simple "have you left" question. Two people who both moved abroad on the same date can land on different sides of the SRT depending on how many UK ties they retained, which changes everything above.

## What This Means in Practice

The work isn't deciding whether you've "left" the UK in a general sense — it's establishing your residency position under the SRT for the specific tax year of the move, claiming split-year treatment correctly if you qualify, and identifying which UK-sourced income still needs reporting going forward. None of that is optional paperwork; it's the difference between a clean exit and years of backdated exposure discovered later.
