## 3 Things Canadian Cross-Border Filers Get Wrong

**1. "I left Canada, so I no longer have to file a Canadian return."**

Departure from Canada does not automatically end your Canadian tax obligations. Your tax residency status is determined by the strength of your residential ties — a spouse in Canada, a home you own, provincial health coverage — not just your physical absence. Many Canadians who think they've become non-residents are still considered factual residents by CRA and owe Canadian tax on worldwide income.

**2. "GST/HST doesn't apply to my digital/SaaS business because my customers are outside Canada."**

Non-resident businesses supplying digital products or services to Canadian consumers may be required to register for GST/HST under the digital economy rules (in effect since July 2021) — even with no physical presence in Canada. The threshold is CAD $30,000 in Canadian sales over any 12-month period. Many non-Canadian SaaS and e-commerce businesses are unregistered and exposed to back-assessments.

**3. "The Canada-US tax treaty means I don't pay tax in both countries."**

The Canada-US Tax Convention prevents double taxation but doesn't eliminate all filing obligations. A Canadian living in the US still needs to file in both countries — the treaty determines who gets first taxing rights and what credits apply. Getting this wrong — particularly on pension income, capital gains, and RRSP distributions — is one of the most expensive mistakes cross-border individuals make.

## Canada Tax Treaties — What They Mean for You

Canada has tax treaties with over 90 countries including the US, UK, and Australia. These treaties:

- Determine which country has primary taxing rights on salary, dividends, capital gains, rental income, and pensions
- Set the rules for determining tax residency when you qualify under both countries' domestic tests (tie-breaker rules)
- Allow you to claim foreign tax credits to offset tax paid in one country against your liability in the other

**The Canada-US treaty** is the most complex for cross-border individuals. Key areas of interaction: RRSP treatment in the US (treaty election required on Form 8891 or 1040), Canadian pension income taxed in Canada first, and the treaty's "saving clause" which means the US still taxes its citizens even when living in Canada.

## Canadian Entity Types — Cross-Border Perspective

| Entity | Canadian tax treatment | Cross-border consideration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canadian Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC) | Eligible for Small Business Deduction (SBD) on first CAD $500K active income | Loses CCPC status if controlled by non-residents — loses SBD eligibility |
| Federal Corporation (Canada Business Corporations Act) | Federal incorporation; must also register extra-provincially to operate in other provinces | Cleanest structure for multi-province and international operations |
| Provincial Corporation | Incorporated in a single province; cheaper to set up | Must register federally or in other provinces to operate outside home province |
| Partnership / LLP | Transparent — income taxed at partner level | US partners in a Canadian partnership face complex cross-border reporting |
| Branch of Foreign Corporation | Canadian branch profits subject to Branch Tax (25% or treaty rate) | Often less tax-efficient than incorporating a Canadian subsidiary |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I still need to file a Canadian return if I've become a non-resident?**

It depends on your Canadian-sourced income. Non-residents with Canadian rental income, pension income (CPP, OAS, RRSP withdrawals), or Canadian employment income are required to file a T1 non-resident return or have Part XIII withholding tax deducted at source. The type of income determines whether you file or withhold — and in many cases, filing is more advantageous.

**When do I need to register for GST/HST?**

You must register when your worldwide taxable revenues exceed CAD $30,000 in any single calendar quarter or over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Non-residents supplying digital services to Canadian consumers must register under the simplified registration system once they exceed CAD $30,000 in Canadian sales. Voluntary early registration is often advisable to claim input tax credits.

**What is the T1135 and do I need to file it?**

The T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) is required for Canadian residents who own specified foreign property with a total cost of more than CAD $100,000 at any point during the year. This includes foreign bank accounts, shares in foreign companies, and foreign real estate not used personally. Failure to file carries significant penalties — up to $2,500 per year for late filing alone.

**Can you handle both my Canadian return and my US return?**

Yes — and coordinating both is where the most value is captured. Treaty elections, RRSP reporting, FBAR obligations, and pension timing all need to be managed together. We handle cross-Canada-US engagements as a single coordinated scope, not two separate filings.

**What's the difference between federal and provincial incorporation in Canada?**

Federal incorporation (under the Canada Business Corporations Act) allows you to operate under the same corporate name across all provinces — but you must still register extra-provincially in each province where you carry on business. Provincial incorporation is cheaper and simpler if you'll only ever operate in one province. For businesses with any growth ambition or multi-province plans, federal incorporation is usually the right starting point.
